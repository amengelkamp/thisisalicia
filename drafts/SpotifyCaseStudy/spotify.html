Product Teardown · Spotify

Spotify built concert discovery. Here's what I'd change.

An analysis of what they shipped, where the product logic runs out, and an interactive prototype exploring the gaps.

Context

Why I have an opinion on this.

I've been a Spotify Premium user for years. Daily listener, regular concert-goer — the kind of user Spotify's own data calls a superfan. I've missed shows from artists I listen to obsessively because I found out too late. I've discovered a tour only after tickets sold out. I've opened Spotify the day after a concert and seen nothing that acknowledged I was there.

That's not a niche problem. Spotify's own stat — "over half of our listeners are unaware of when their favourite artists are in town" — describes me. I built this prototype because I think the data Spotify already has should solve it, and the product logic to do so is not that complicated.

My background is payment processing — owning the backend layer where user intent either converts or abandons. That gives me a specific lens on conversion friction. Concert discovery has the same shape: a user who would buy a ticket doesn't, because the right signal didn't reach them at the right moment. This case study is about closing that gap.

What They Built

Spotify's version is good. Its most powerful surface isn't consistently deployed.

Spotify launched live event discovery in 2022 and has kept building it. Today, open.spotify.com/concerts is a genuinely solid product: personalised recommendations ranked by listening history, radius filtering, date and genre filters, a horizontal scroll row of artist cards. The core insight — your listening data is the best possible signal for concert relevance — is right, and they executed it.

Calling this a purely pull product is no longer accurate. Spotify surfaces shows in the Now Playing view while you're listening, pushes the Concerts Near You playlist to your library every Wednesday, and with Reserved, proactively holds tickets for identified superfans. The push mechanisms exist. The specific gap is the home screen row: Now Playing catches you mid-session, a moment of music focus rather than purchase intent. The playlist requires you to discover it first. The home screen row catches you at any moment, unprompted. That surface existed in 2022. It isn't consistently shown today, at least not outside the US.

Three things the current product leaves open. The radius filter is a hard cutoff with no documented exception logic — an artist in your top five playing their only European date 600 km away gets silently excluded the same way a band you've never heard of would. Sold-out handling isn't documented anywhere: whether Spotify shows sold-out events with a badge (still useful signal — tells you the artist has demand near you) or removes them entirely, I couldn't confirm. Festival deduplication isn't documented either: a festival with four of your top artists could plausibly appear four times as separate cards, or once — I couldn't find evidence either way.

What I'd Change

Three bets worth making differently.

01

Make the home screen row the primary surface, not an inconsistently deployed one

Spotify already pushes concerts through Now Playing and the weekly playlist — but both require prior behaviour. Now Playing only fires if you're actively listening to the artist. The playlist requires you to know it exists. The home screen row is different: it intercepts users who have no intent to look for concerts. That's a meaningfully larger addressable audience, which is why it's the highest-leverage surface. The likely reason Spotify scaled it back after 2022 is the clutter problem — a row that shows up when you have nothing nearby is just noise. The fix is already in this prototype: the row only renders when there's at least one match within radius, and disappears silently when there isn't. No match, no row, no clutter. That design removes the objection.

02

iOS gives you one shot at the permission dialog — spend it when the value is already visible

On iOS, once a user dismisses the native notification dialog with "Don't Allow," you can never surface it again. That makes the soft ask — Spotify's own in-app prompt before the OS dialog fires — critical. It needs to land at the highest-intent moment possible. The first appearance of the concerts row is that moment: the user has just discovered Spotify knows about a show near them. "Want to be notified about this?" has an obvious answer. Ask at onboarding and you're spending your one OS dialog on a user who has experienced nothing yet. Two steps: Spotify earns the intent, the OS grants the permission.

03

The radius is a blunt instrument — add logic for the shows worth traveling for

If an artist is in your top 5 by listening time and has three or fewer European dates, show the concert regardless of radius. Top 5 is a deliberate threshold: it approximates the artists you know every lyric to and would plan around — not just artists you like. Top 10 is too wide; most people wouldn't travel internationally for their tenth-favourite act. ≤3 dates means the tour is genuinely rare — miss this one and you're likely waiting years. Spotify's own Reserved bet makes the underlying logic explicit: superfans identified by listening depth have higher purchase intent regardless of normal friction. Travel distance is one form of friction. The exception rule applies that same assumption to discovery. One design constraint matters: the label "Only European Show" is non-negotiable. Systems should be transparent when they break their own rules — the user sees exactly why this show appeared outside their radius, and can decide whether to act on it.

Design Decisions

Five places where this prototype diverges from Spotify's version.

1

Medium-term listening, not all-time

All-time totals are dominated by whatever you were obsessed with years ago. But too short a window and one obsessive week skews the whole ranking. Spotify's API offers three ranges: roughly 4 weeks, 6 months, and all-time. Six months hits the right balance — recent enough to reflect who you're actually listening to now, stable enough to filter out spikes. It's also what the API directly supports, so there's no approximation needed.

2

The "Only European Show" exception rule

If an artist is in the user's top 5 by listening time and has three or fewer European tour dates, show the concert regardless of radius. A user who listens to Phoebe Bridgers for 200 hours a year would probably travel to Berlin for her only European show — the radius shouldn't silently hide that. The label "Only European Show" does the persuasion work; the system never expands radius without telling you.

3

One card per festival

A festival with four of your favourite artists showing up as four separate cards would create noise and bury single-artist shows. One festival card, ranked by combined listening time of all matching artists, with the top three named on the card. The full lineup lives on the detail page.

4

Sold-out shows stay visible

Hiding sold-out shows removes real signal — a sold-out show tells you the artist has demand near you, and resale tickets often exist. Sold-out entries get a greyed treatment and a badge. If total results exceed 10, a toggle appears to hide them. The user controls this, not the algorithm.

5

The detail page earns the redirect

Tapping "Tickets kaufen" sends the user to Ticketmaster. But the detail page comes first: date, venue, distance, support acts, map, price. The user should arrive at Ticketmaster already decided — not still figuring out whether they want to go. The redirect is the last step, not the first.

Technical Approach

Two APIs, one meaningful combination.

The listening signal comes from Spotify's Web API — specifically /me/top/artists with time_range=medium_term (approximately 6 months — recent enough to reflect current taste, stable enough to smooth out single-week spikes). Concert data comes from the Ticketmaster Discovery API or Bandsintown — Spotify's concert data is not exposed in their public API.

The matching logic is straightforward: fetch the user's top 50 artists, call the concert API for each, filter by radius, sort by date with listening rank as a tiebreaker. The "Only European Show" exception runs as a post-filter pass on the top-5 artists.

Note: Spotify has been progressively restricting public API access since 2024. Verify current endpoint availability before building — the endpoints referenced here were available at time of writing but the landscape is shifting.

What I'd Measure

How we know it's working.

1

Click-through rate on the concerts row

% of users who see the row and tap at least one card. Baseline against similar discovery rows (artist radio, playlist recommendations). If CTR is below baseline, the ranking signal isn't personal enough.

2

Notification opt-in rate

% of users who see the prompt and tap "Yes, notify me." Placed at the highest-intent moment — this number sets the ceiling for how many users benefit from the follow-on notification system. Target: above 40%.

3

Ticketmaster redirect rate

% of detail page views that result in a tap on "Buy tickets." This measures whether the detail page earns the redirect. Low rate = the page isn't convincing enough or the user decided against it after seeing price/distance.

4

Radius adjustment rate

% of users on the full concerts page who change the default 150 km radius. High rate = the default is wrong for a significant segment. Low rate = 150 km is working. Segment by city size — rural users likely need a wider default.

What's Next

The MVP leaves one obvious gap.

The post-show experience is completely absent. A user who tapped "Tickets kaufen," went to the concert, and came back to Spotify the next day sees nothing different. That's a missed moment.

→

Post-show setlist replay

If the user attended a show (inferred from location data, or from a Ticketmaster data-sharing agreement), surface a playlist of what the artist actually played that night. The fandom spike is highest in the 24 hours after a show — that's the moment to deepen the relationship, not a week later.

→

Friend activity on concerts

Spotify already shows what friends are listening to. Extending that to "3 friends are going to this" adds social proof to the ticket decision — the highest-converting trigger for live event attendance.

→

In-app ticket purchase

The MVP redirects to Ticketmaster — necessary for launch speed, but it breaks the flow and loses attribution. A deeper partnership or white-label integration would keep the purchase in-app and give Spotify direct conversion data.
